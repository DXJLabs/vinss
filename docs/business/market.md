# VINSS Market

> **VINSS should define its market from the recurring job it solves, not from the total size of crypto, Web3, freelancing, payments, or fraud.**

**Status:** Market strategy baseline  
**Scope:** VINSS  
**Purpose:** Define the market VINSS is actually attempting to serve, separate market context from addressable demand, establish a defensible TAM/SAM/SOM framework, identify beachhead hypotheses, and specify what evidence is still required before making market-size claims.

---

## 1. Market Thesis

VINSS operates at the intersection of several large economic activities:

```text
direct digital deals;
private negotiation;
crypto / stablecoin settlement;
freelance and digital work;
peer-to-peer commerce;
OTC transactions;
wallet-native payments;
escrow / Rekber;
transaction evidence.
```

That does **not** mean the VINSS market is the sum of all of them.

The relevant market is narrower:

> **Two-party transactions where negotiation matters, trust is incomplete, value is at risk, privacy has value, and the parties need a clearer path from agreement to settlement.**

This definition excludes many transactions that technically could use VINSS but have little reason to.

For example:

```text
trusted friend sends $10
→ probably does not need VINSS

customer buys standard product from major marketplace
→ platform checkout may already solve the problem

simple wallet transfer with no future obligation
→ VINSS may add unnecessary friction
```

VINSS becomes more relevant when:

```text
terms must be negotiated;
one side must perform before final settlement;
the counterparty is new or pseudonymous;
deal context is sensitive;
evidence matters;
refund / release conditions matter.
```

---

## 2. Market Context Is Not TAM

VINSS should explicitly distinguish:

```text
MARKET CONTEXT
```

from:

```text
ADDRESSABLE VINSS MARKET
```

Large numbers can show that the surrounding economic rails are meaningful.

They do not prove that all of that activity is available to VINSS.

Examples of numbers that should **not** automatically become VINSS TAM:

```text
total crypto market capitalization;
total stablecoin transaction volume;
total freelance labor market;
total e-commerce GMV;
total fraud losses;
total NFT trading volume;
total DeFi TVL.
```

The rule is:

> **A market number belongs in VINSS TAM only when there is a credible path from that activity to the specific job VINSS performs and a credible mechanism for VINSS to capture revenue from it.**

## 3. External Market Signals

Public evidence supports several conditions that make the VINSS thesis worth testing.

These are **context signals**, not proof of VINSS demand.

### 3.1 Stablecoins are meaningful settlement infrastructure

Visa reported more than:

```text
$272B
```

in circulating stablecoin supply and approximately:

```text
$10.2T
```

in adjusted stablecoin transaction volume over the preceding 12 months in its stablecoin analytics work.

The adjusted methodology attempts to remove some bot, exchange-rebalancing, and smart-contract-related activity rather than treating all on-chain transfer volume as payment activity.

This supports the claim that stablecoins are substantial economic rails.

It does **not** prove that the transactions represented by that volume need VINSS.

### 3.2 Identifiable stablecoin payment activity is growing

Artemis' 2025 stablecoin payments study attributed approximately:

```text
$136B
```

of stablecoin payments from January 2023 through August 2025 across its researched sample and estimates.

The report estimated an August 2025 annualized run rate of approximately:

```text
$122B
```

with B2B the largest category in the sample.

This is relevant because VINSS is ultimately a settlement-coordination product rather than a speculative trading product.

Again:

```text
stablecoin payments
≠
VINSS market
```

but they demonstrate that wallet-native settlement increasingly exists outside pure token speculation.

### 3.3 Crypto adoption is geographically broad

Chainalysis' 2025 Global Crypto Adoption Index ranked countries across retail, centralized, DeFi, and institutional activity.

Its 2025 results placed several APAC markets near the top and ranked Indonesia seventh overall.

Chainalysis also estimated APAC on-chain value received grew strongly in the period it measured.

This is useful as a distribution and adoption signal.

It does not prove that users in those countries want a Private Deal Room or will pay VINSS fees.

### 3.4 Online gig work is economically meaningful but difficult to size precisely

The World Bank's *Working Without Borders* research estimated a wide range of approximately:

```text
154M–435M
```

online gig workers globally depending on methodology and definition.

The wide range itself is important.

It demonstrates that even a well-studied adjacent market can be difficult to size accurately.

VINSS should therefore avoid taking the largest available gig-work estimate and presenting it as a customer count.

## 4. The Market Problem VINSS Targets

The common denominator across VINSS candidate markets is not the industry label.

It is a transaction pattern.

A VINSS-relevant transaction often looks like:

```text
two parties meet
        ↓
negotiate in chat
        ↓
terms change
        ↓
one version becomes final
        ↓
someone must pay or fund
        ↓
someone must fulfill
        ↓
the other side must verify
        ↓
value is released / refunded / disputed
        ↓
evidence may be needed later
```

Today this may happen across:

```text
Telegram / Discord / DM
+
wallet
+
screenshots
+
manual notes
+
direct payment
+
middleman / separate escrow
```

VINSS is targeting the economic activity where that fragmentation creates enough:

```text
risk;
ambiguity;
privacy concern;
operational friction;
or evidence burden
```

to justify switching behavior and paying.

## 5. The VINSS Market Filter

A transaction should pass several filters before it belongs in the realistic serviceable market.

### Filter 1 — Direct or bilateral

There are identifiable counterparties.

### Filter 2 — Negotiated

Terms are not completely standardized before interaction.

### Filter 3 — Economic value at risk

Failure matters enough that protection has value.

### Filter 4 — Trust gap

The parties do not have complete trust or enforceability.

### Filter 5 — Deferred or conditional settlement

Something must happen before final value movement is legitimate.

### Filter 6 — Verification need

The product can improve how completion is determined.

### Filter 7 — Privacy relevance

At least some transaction context is better kept private.

### Filter 8 — Wallet / rail compatibility

The settlement asset and participant behavior can realistically use VINSS.

### Filter 9 — Willingness to pay

The value of protection exceeds the perceived cost and friction.

The serviceable market is the intersection of these filters.

Not the union.

## 6. Market Layers

VINSS should use three separate market layers.

### Layer A — Context Market

All surrounding activity that demonstrates the environment exists.

Examples:

```text
stablecoin settlement;
crypto-native commerce;
online freelance work;
digital-goods trade;
P2P transactions;
OTC activity;
NFT transfers;
bounties.
```

This is useful for understanding macro direction.

It is too broad for a revenue claim.

### Layer B — Serviceable Market

Transactions that match the VINSS job:

```text
bilateral
+
negotiated
+
meaningful value
+
trust gap
+
settlement need
+
verification fit
+
privacy relevance
+
supported rails
```

This is the market VINSS could plausibly serve with the product.

### Layer C — Beachhead Market

One narrow segment where VINSS can demonstrate:

```text
repeated pain;
repeatable acquisition;
real paid usage;
high product fit;
reasonable support cost;
repeat settlement.
```

VINSS has not yet validated this layer.

## 7. TAM Should Be Defined From Eligible Deal Activity

The future TAM should not be calculated as:

```text
global crypto volume × 2%
```

or:

```text
global freelance market × arbitrary market share
```

A more defensible long-term TAM framework is:

```text
Eligible annual paid actions
×
average VINSS action revenue

+

Eligible annual protected GMV
×
effective Rekber take rate
```

Conceptually:

```text
TAM Revenue
=
Usage Revenue Opportunity
+
Settlement Revenue Opportunity
```

Where:

```text
Usage Revenue Opportunity
=
eligible rooms
+
messages
+
Offer actions
+
Fulfillment / Review actions
```

and:

```text
Settlement Revenue Opportunity
=
eligible Rekber GMV
×
effective take rate
```

This requires actual segment data.

Until those inputs exist, VINSS should not publish a precise TAM as though it were measured.

## 8. Bottom-Up Sizing Is More Useful Than Headline TAM

At VINSS's current stage, bottom-up economics are more useful.

The current business-planning base case assumes, per:

```text
100 monthly active users
```

approximately:

```text
50 new rooms;
1,000 messages;
200 Offer / Counter / Accept actions;
20 completed Rekber;
$100 average deal value;
$2,000 monthly GMV;
20 Fulfillment actions;
20 Review actions.
```

At public baseline pricing, that model produces approximately:

```text
$258.50 monthly gross VINSS revenue
```

before variable costs.

This model answers:

> **What could a cohort of active users be worth if they behave this way?**

It does not answer:

> **How many users exist in the market?**

Both questions are needed.

They should not be confused.

## 9. Revenue Opportunity Formula

For a candidate segment:

```text
Monthly Revenue Opportunity
=
Active Users
×
Average Paid Usage Revenue per User

+

Monthly Protected GMV
×
Effective Rekber Take Rate
```

A more detailed form:

```text
Revenue
=
(Rooms × Room Fee)
+
(Messages × Message Fee)
+
(Offer Actions × Offer Fee)
+
(Fulfillment Actions × Fulfillment Fee)
+
(Review Actions × Review Fee)
+
Σ Rekber Fees
```

Then calculate:

```text
Contribution
=
Revenue
-
Variable transaction cost
```

Only after that should fixed infrastructure and wider overhead be considered.

This makes market attractiveness sensitive not merely to user count, but to:

```text
frequency;
deal value;
transaction complexity;
variable cost;
retention.
```

## 10. Why a Smaller Segment Can Be a Better Market

Suppose Segment A has:

```text
1,000,000 potential users
```

but each user:

```text
makes one relevant deal per year;
has low willingness to pay;
is expensive to acquire.
```

Segment B may have only:

```text
50,000 potential users
```

but each user:

```text
makes multiple deals monthly;
has $500–$5,000 at risk;
already pays for protection;
is concentrated in reachable communities.
```

Segment B can be a much stronger beachhead.

Therefore:

> **Frequency × pain × economic value × reachability matters more than raw population.**

## 11. Candidate Market: Freelance / Services

### Why it is relevant

The workflow naturally contains:

```text
negotiation;
scope;
price;
deadline;
work;
review;
payment.
```

The trust problem can exist on both sides.

### Potential strengths

```text
repeat behavior;
clear Accepted Agreement;
natural Fulfillment;
meaningful payment risk;
easy explanation of Rekber.
```

### Market evidence

Online gig work is globally significant, but broad gig-worker estimates include many workers who:

```text
use marketplaces;
do not use crypto;
do not need private settlement;
do not negotiate direct bilateral deals.
```

Therefore the relevant VINSS freelance market is a subset:

> **Direct or off-platform service transactions where crypto/stablecoin settlement and bilateral protection are realistic.**

### Key unknowns

```text
How many eligible freelancers transact directly?

How many use crypto / stablecoins?

How frequently do they need protection?

Would they move negotiation into VINSS?

Would client and freelancer both accept the fee model?
```

Examples:

```text
source code;
licenses;
design assets;
datasets;
templates;
research;
private files;
software deliverables.
```

### Why it may fit

Digital-goods deals can have:

```text
fast settlement;
encrypted delivery;
clear delivery evidence;
repeat sellers;
cross-border buyers.
```

VINSS may be able to prove:

```text
delivery occurred
```

without claiming:

```text
quality was objectively correct.
```

### Potential market advantage

Compared with physical goods, digital fulfillment reduces dependence on:

```text
shipping APIs;
couriers;
inspection logistics.
```

That can make the segment operationally easier for an early product.

### Key unknowns

```text
Where do these direct sellers operate?

How much volume is outside marketplaces?

What percentage already uses stablecoins?

How often does non-payment or bad delivery matter?

What fee is acceptable?
```

Examples:

```text

designers;

```

### Why it may be strategically attractive

Participants may already have:

```text

pseudonymous relationships;
cross-border work;
Telegram / Discord workflows;
tolerance for on-chain interaction.
```

This reduces adoption steps compared with mainstream users.

### Risk

Crypto-native users may already be comfortable with direct transfers and informal trust networks.

The fact that they know how to use wallets does not mean they want an additional Deal Room.

## 14. Candidate Market: OTC / Bilateral Token Trade

### Potential strengths

```text
high deal values;
privacy sensitivity;
settlement sequencing risk;
strong consequences of error.
```

Even low user counts can represent meaningful GMV.

### Product opportunity

For on-chain ↔ on-chain transactions, objective verification may reduce settlement ambiguity.

For crypto ↔ fiat:

```text
bank receipt
```

is not natively visible on-chain.

That increases:

```text
evidence;
confirmation;
dispute;
operational complexity.
```

### Market risk

Professional OTC desks already solve:

```text
counterparty sourcing;
trust;
pricing;
settlement;
compliance;
human resolution.
```

VINSS would need to establish why software-only or software-first coordination is better for a particular subset.

## 15. Candidate Market: Bounties

Bounties can have:

```text
reward;
criteria;
deadline;
submission;
review.
```

This aligns well with VINSS.

### Attractive property

Some criteria can be relatively objective:

```text
issue closed;
test passed;
specific artifact delivered.
```

### Risk

A public bounty market also requires:

```text
discovery;
applicant management;
spam control;
selection.
```

VINSS currently focuses on the deal lifecycle.

Therefore the initial serviceable bounty market is more likely:

> **A bounty relationship where the parties or participant have already been selected.**

not a full public bounty marketplace.

## 16. Candidate Market: NFT / On-Chain Asset Deals

### Why it is technically attractive

VINSS can potentially verify:

```text
contract;
token ID;
recipient;
ownership.
```

This is one of the cleanest verification environments.

### Market question

Technical fit does not establish market attractiveness.

The segment must still demonstrate:

```text
enough direct P2P volume;
private negotiation need;
meaningful deal values;
willingness to pay;
repeat behavior.
```

A cyclical or shrinking market can still be a weak beachhead even if the product flow is elegant.

## 17. Candidate Market: Physical Goods

### Why it is intuitive

Buyer and seller protection is easy to understand.

### Why it is operationally difficult

Relevant truth often depends on:

```text
shipping;
delivery;
condition;
inspection;
external evidence.
```

The blockchain cannot directly determine whether:

```text
a laptop arrived damaged
```

or:

```text
the box contained the correct item.
```

This creates dispute and integration requirements.

Physical goods may therefore represent a large theoretical market but a more expensive serviceable market for an early VINSS product.

## 18. Candidate Market: Wallets and Marketplaces

This is a different market model.

The customer may become:

```text
wallet;
marketplace;
community platform;
payments product;
crypto application.
```

rather than only the end participant.

### Partner value proposition

VINSS could potentially provide:

```text
private deal coordination;
Offer infrastructure;
deal-linked Rekber;
settlement state;
evidence / certificate primitives.
```

### Why this can matter

Distribution is one of the hardest problems for standalone products.

Embedding into an existing user environment could lower acquisition friction.

### Why it is not automatically easier

B2B buyers may require:

```text
API maturity;
SLA;
security review;
support;
integration work;
compliance review;
custom economics.
```

One large integration can consume substantial engineering capacity.

B2B therefore remains a separate market hypothesis.

## 20. Product Fit vs Market Attractiveness

VINSS should score candidate markets on two dimensions separately.

### Product Fit

Can VINSS solve the transaction well?

Criteria:

```text
clear roles;
clear Fulfillment;
verification fit;
privacy relevance;
settlement compatibility.
```

### Market Attractiveness

Is the segment worth pursuing?

Criteria:

```text
pain;
frequency;
deal value;
willingness to pay;
market growth;
reachability;
competition;
retention;
support cost.
```

A segment can have:

```text
high product fit
+
low market attractiveness
```

or:

```text
large market
+
poor product fit.
```

Neither should automatically become the beachhead.

## 21. Beachhead Selection Framework

VINSS should choose its first market using evidence across at least:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Pain | Does failure create real financial or operational loss? |
| Frequency | Does the transaction repeat often? |
| Deal value | Is enough value at risk to justify VINSS fees? |
| Trust gap | Are counterparties often new or pseudonymous? |
| Privacy | Does private context matter? |
| Verification fit | Can VINSS improve determination of success? |
| Wallet readiness | Can users operate the required settlement rails? |
| Reachability | Can VINSS acquire these users repeatedly? |
| Willingness to pay | Do they already pay for protection or accept transaction fees? |
| Retention | Is there a natural reason to use VINSS again? |
| Support cost | Can the product serve the segment without excessive manual work? |

No segment should be declared the beachhead before these are tested.

A reasonable sequence is:

### Priority A — Digitally native bilateral deals

```text

digital goods;
selected bounties;
crypto-native vendor work.
```

Why test early:

```text
wallet readiness;
digital Fulfillment;
repeatable flow;
lower physical-world integration burden.
```

### Priority B — Objective on-chain deals

```text
NFT;
selected token / asset trades.
```

Why test:

```text
strong verification model;
clear demonstration of deterministic settlement.
```

### Priority C — High-value off-chain / hybrid deals

```text
OTC fiat leg;
physical goods;
complex Custom Deals.
```

Why later:

```text
higher verification and dispute complexity.
```

This ordering is a **validation strategy**, not a market conclusion.

Real customer evidence can reverse it.

However, geographic priorities can matter for distribution.

Recent crypto-adoption evidence shows strong activity across:

```text

North America;
Europe;
Latin America;
other emerging markets.
```

Chainalysis' 2025 index placed several Asian markets among the leading adoption countries, including Indonesia.

This suggests APAC can be a reasonable region for customer discovery.

It does not mean:

```text
Indonesia = VINSS beachhead
```

without direct evidence.

Geographic selection should consider:

```text

stablecoin usage;
language;
regulation;
```

That does not mean its market should be defined only as:

```text

arrive through a partner;

```

However, near-term distribution can still benefit from:

```text

ecosystem partners;
wallet integrations;
hackathon / developer networks.
```

The distinction is:

```text
Infrastructure ecosystem
≠
ultimate customer market
```

## 26. Market Network Effects

VINSS does not initially require a classic public marketplace network effect.

A Deal Room involves two counterparties.

The product can grow through:

```text
invites;
repeat counterparties;
referrals;
Settlement SBT reputation;
wallet / marketplace integration.
```

Potential loop:

```text
Alice uses VINSS
        ↓
invites Bob
        ↓
Bob completes a real deal
        ↓
Bob understands VINSS
        ↓
Bob later creates a room with Carol
```

This is a possible **transaction-driven distribution loop**.

It should be measured rather than assumed.

## 27. Referral as a Market Signal

VINSS already conceptualizes Invite as the referral mechanism.

Referral quality matters more than Invite creation count.

Useful market signals include:

```text
invite accepted;
referred wallet performs first real action;
referred wallet completes first Rekber;
referred wallet later initiates a new room.
```

This distinguishes:

```text
link sharing
```

from:

```text
actual market pull.
```

A high rate of successful deal-driven referrals would strengthen evidence that VINSS has organic distribution.

## 29. Signals of a Real Beachhead

A beachhead becomes more credible when a coherent segment shows:

```text
same recurring problem;
similar existing workaround;
similar reason to choose VINSS;
repeat paid usage;
similar acquisition channel;
repeat referrals;
acceptable support burden.
```

For example:

```text
20 unrelated people use VINSS once
```

is weaker evidence than:

```text
10 digital-goods sellers
use VINSS repeatedly
for the same type of transaction
and invite their buyers.
```

Concentration can be valuable evidence.

VINSS can say:

> **The surrounding payment and crypto rails are economically significant and continue to support wallet-native transaction behavior.**

VINSS can say:

> **Candidate markets include crypto-native services, digital goods, bilateral asset trades, bounties, and partner-embedded deal flows.**

VINSS should also say:

> **The initial beachhead, addressable user count, willingness to pay, and repeatable acquisition channel remain validation questions.**

## 34. Initial Customer Discovery Targets

Rather than interviewing a random crypto audience, VINSS should seek participants with recent transaction behavior.

Strong interview profile:

```text
completed ≥3 direct deals recently;
used chat for negotiation;
used wallet or stablecoin settlement;
worked with at least one new counterparty;
experienced payment / delivery uncertainty;
can describe the last deal in detail.
```

Ask about:

```text
the last actual transaction
```

before asking:

```text
Would you use VINSS?
```

Past behavior is stronger evidence than hypothetical enthusiasm.

```text
-
Variable Costs
=
Economic Opportunity
```

This is more useful than a giant top-down TAM number with no path to revenue.

## 36. Scenario-Based SOM

Before a validated market exists, VINSS can use scenario planning.

For example:

```text
1,000 active users
```

under the current public-price base usage model would imply approximately:

```text
$20,000 monthly GMV;
$2,585 monthly gross revenue
```

before the updated variable cost assumptions.

At:

```text
10,000 active users
```

the same behavior implies approximately:

```text
$200,000 monthly GMV;
$25,850 monthly gross revenue.
```

These figures show the economics of a hypothetical captured user base.

They do **not** prove that acquiring or retaining those users is possible.

Therefore they are:

```text
SOM SCENARIOS
```

not:

```text
SOM EVIDENCE.
```

## 37. What Would Justify a Formal TAM/SAM/SOM Number

VINSS should only publish a more formal market estimate after obtaining enough segment-specific inputs.

Required inputs include:

```text
number of reachable participants;
eligible deal frequency;
average / median deal size;
percentage of deals needing protection;
wallet / stablecoin readiness;
expected paid action frequency;
expected Rekber conversion;
willingness to pay;
realistic geographic coverage.
```

Then:

### TAM

All eligible deals/users matching the job definition across supported long-term markets.

### SAM

Eligible deals/users VINSS can serve with:

```text
current settlement rails;
current Deal Types;
current verification capabilities;
supported regions / infrastructure.
```

### SOM

The portion VINSS can realistically acquire within a defined period using identified distribution channels.

Without those inputs, a precise number would create false certainty.

## 38. Market Risks

### Market is real but the workflow is not painful enough

Users may accept fragmented tools.

### Protection is valuable but users prefer existing marketplaces

VINSS may solve settlement but not discovery.

### Users want escrow but not paid messaging

Revenue mix could differ materially from the current base case.

### High-value users demand stronger dispute resolution

Operational cost could rise.

### Low-value users cannot support the fee floor

Small deals may be uneconomic.

### Token incentives distort apparent adoption

Campaign users can look like market demand.

### Privacy is valued in theory but does not change purchase behavior

Privacy preference must translate into actual product choice.

### Wallet friction reduces conversion

Technical capability is not equivalent to mainstream usability.

### B2B distribution takes too long

Partner sales may be slower than direct user acquisition.

## 39. Market Strategy Principles

1. **Do not use total crypto volume as VINSS TAM.**
2. **Do not use fraud losses as revenue opportunity.**
3. **Market context and addressable market are different.**
4. **The addressable unit is an eligible deal, not merely a wallet.**
5. **The strongest segment has repeated pain, not merely theoretical fit.**
6. **Frequency and deal value can matter more than raw user count.**
7. **Product fit and market attractiveness must be evaluated separately.**
8. **Verification complexity affects market economics.**
9. **A technically elegant Deal Type is not automatically a good beachhead.**
10. **A large adjacent industry is not automatically serviceable.**
11. **Bottom-up user and GMV models should drive planning.**
12. **Real paid behavior should progressively replace assumptions.**
13. **Referrals should be measured through meaningful downstream activity.**
14. **Starknet is infrastructure and an early distribution ecosystem, not the entire market definition.**
15. **The beachhead remains a hypothesis until repeated usage reveals it.**

## 40. Market Thesis

VINSS does not need to win:

```text
all messaging;
all escrow;
all freelancing;
all payments;
all crypto transactions.
```

It needs to become the preferred workflow for a narrower recurring job:

> **When two parties have a direct deal, do not fully trust each other, need to negotiate before settlement, care about privacy, and need clear rules for fulfillment and payment.**

If VINSS can prove repeated paid use for that job in one reachable segment, the product can expand from there.

That is a stronger market foundation than claiming a percentage of a giant adjacent industry.

## 42. Evidence Date Discipline

Market data changes.

VINSS documentation should attach a date to external numbers and refresh them when the market strategy is materially updated.

A source can support:

```text
market context at a point in time
```

but should not become a permanent constant in the product model.

The market model should ultimately be driven by VINSS's own:

```text
real users;
real deal frequency;
real GMV;
real fees;
real retention;
real referrals.
```
